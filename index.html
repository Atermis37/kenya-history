01DEEP TIME

BEFORE THE NATIONS

The cradle.

Long before modern borders, the landscapes of present-day Kenya were home to ancient human ancestors. The fossil-rich sites around Lake Turkana have helped researchers understand human evolution.

01

Koobi Fora
On the east shore of Lake Turkana, its sediments span about five to one million years. According to the National Museums of Kenya, it produced a Homo erectus skull about 1.6 million years old and, by 1994, about 200 hominid specimens. It is part of Lake Turkana National Parks, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. [1]

02

Turkana Boy
Found at Nariokotome, West Turkana, in 1984 by Kamoya Kimeu. The skeleton belongs to a Homo erectus boy of about eight or nine who lived around 1.6 million years ago. About 40 percent of it is preserved, and it has taught scientists about the body size, shape and growth of Homo erectus. [6]

02MIGRATIONS

MOVEMENT AND SETTLEMENT

Peoples on
the move.

Kenya's people speak languages from three families: Cushitic, Nilotic and Bantu. [16]

Cushitic-speaking herders have long lived in the arid north and east, and earlier Cushitic peoples in the west and highlands were absorbed or displaced as others moved in. Highland Nilotes such as the Kalenjin expanded across the Rift Valley around the middle of the last millennium, and Bantu speakers reached the Lake Victoria region by about the eleventh century. Oral histories place the ancestors of the Kikuyu and Kamba in central Kenya from about the fifteenth century. [16]

03

Still debated
Historians have often linked the Bantu-speaking peoples of the eastern highlands to a migration from a place called Shungwaya, but a Journal of African History study re-examines that account, and sources date the Nilotic arrival differently. Treat every date here as approximate. [16]

03COASTAL HISTORY

THE INDIAN OCEAN WORLD

The coast
looks outward.

Swahili city-states such as Gedi, Lamu and Mombasa traded across the Indian Ocean with Arabia, Persia, India and China. Swahili means people of the coast, and Islam became central to Swahili identity. [17]

04

Gedi and Lamu
Gedi was one of the most important Swahili cities from the 10th to the 17th centuries. Goods from China, Persia, India and Venice have been found there, and its exports included gold, ivory and enslaved people. [9] Lamu Old Town is the oldest and best-preserved Swahili settlement in East Africa and has been continuously inhabited for over 700 years. [10]

05

Fort Jesus
The Portuguese built the fort in Mombasa from 1593, on a coral ridge at the harbour entrance, to a design by the Italian engineer Batista Cairato. Omani Arabs attacked it from 1696 to 1698. UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site in 2011. [2]

04LATE 19TH–20TH CENTURY

COLONIAL OCCUPATION

The railway
and the colony.

British colonial rule transformed land ownership, labour, political authority and everyday life.

06

From protectorate to colony
Britain proclaimed its protectorate on 1 July 1895, and Fort Jesus in Mombasa was converted into a prison. [2] On 23 July 1920 the inland territory was annexed as the Colony of Kenya, a Crown colony without responsible government. [15]

07

The Uganda Railway
Construction began on 30 May 1896. The line reached Nairobi in 1899, where a railway depot and camp became the start of the city, and was completed at Kisumu (then Port Florence) on 19 December 1901. Kenya Railways says about 2,500 lives were lost, a figure from the railway's own history. [11]

05RESISTANCE AND INDEPENDENCE

THE FIGHT FOR FREEDOM

The struggle
for freedom.

Resistance to colonial rule took many forms across the country. The Mau Mau movement, which began among the Kikuyu, became a major part of the struggle that led to independence on 12 December 1963. [3] [13]

08

Koitalel arap Samoei
The Nandi orkoiyot, a sacred and political leader, led resistance to British rule. He was killed on 19 October 1905 by Colonel Richard Meinertzhagen at a meeting he had been invited to as a truce. Meinertzhagen admitted the killing in his memoir. [14]

09

Mekatilili wa Menza
In 1913 she rallied the Giriama against hut taxes, forced labour and British-appointed headmen, and she was arrested in October of that year. Giriama reclaimed their sacred kaya in 1919. [12]

10

The Emergency
The Mau Mau movement was banned in 1950. In October 1952 the colonial government declared a state of emergency. Britannica reports more than 11,000 rebels killed by the end of 1956, about 100 Europeans and 2,000 African loyalists, and more than 20,000 Kikuyu held in detention camps. [3]

11

The 2013 settlement
On 6 June 2013 the British Government announced an out-of-court settlement: a statement of regret, damages of KSh 330,000 each to 5,228 claimants, and a contribution to a monument for victims of colonial-era torture. [4]

A COUNTRY STILL BECOMING

A republic
of 47.

Kenya became fully independent on 12 December 1963 and a republic a year later, with Jomo Kenyatta as its first president. [13] The 2010 Constitution declares Kenya a sovereign, multi-party republic divided into 47 counties. [5]

12

What the Constitution says
Kiswahili is the national language, and Kiswahili and English are the official languages. There is no state religion. Article 11 recognises culture as the foundation of the nation. Madaraka Day (1 June), Mashujaa Day (20 October) and Jamhuri Day (12 December) are national days. [5]

13

Wangari Maathai
Born in Nyeri in 1940, she was the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate. She won the 2004 Nobel Peace Prize. The Green Belt Movement, which grew from her 1976 idea of planting trees with women's groups, had helped plant more than 20 million trees by the time of the award. [7]

14

M-Pesa
Safaricom officially launched the mobile money service on 6 March 2007. By November that year it had more than a million active users. [8]
